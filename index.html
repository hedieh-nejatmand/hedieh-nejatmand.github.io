The Big Picture

Think about the first time you use a new piece of software. You’re trying to remember the instructions, find the right controls, and figure out what happened when you clicked the wrong button. By the time you find the right menu, you might have already forgotten what you were supposed to click. That’s an everyday example of cognitive load, the mental demands a task places on you.

Working memory plays a central role in how we learn under cognitive load. In my experiments, I vary what people have to keep in mind while they learn, placing different demands on verbal, visuospatial, or nonverbal auditory working memory. How do these different kinds of cognitive load affect learning, and what helps people learn under each?

I explore this question through category learning. When people learn to group images or sounds, I can see how well they do during practice and how well they apply what they’ve learned to new examples. I’m especially interested in how labeling, feedback, and task design can support both learning and generalization. I use a combination of ecologically valid research paradigms, quantitative methods, and computational modeling.

How does working memory shape what we learn from images and sounds?

I study how demands on working memory affect the way people learn categories from images and sounds. I’m interested in how people learn during practice and how well they can use what they’ve learned to classify new examples.

Can learning help us remember what we see?

I study whether learning to recognize categories helps people hold visual details in mind. This project explores how what we’ve learned shapes how well we use visual working memory.

What do our expectations have to do with cognitive performance?

Before we start a task, we often have a sense of how difficult it will be. I study how those expectations relate to how well we perform, particularly when a task relies on working memory.

Are people with more vivid mental imagery better at learning categories?

In our CogSci 2025 study, we asked whether people who reported stronger visual or auditory imagery were better at learning categories in the corresponding sense. We found little evidence of a relationship in either vision or hearing. Read the paper.
